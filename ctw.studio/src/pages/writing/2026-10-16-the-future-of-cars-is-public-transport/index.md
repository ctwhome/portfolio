People wanted more horses, and hell, the Ford Model T delivered. Horses under the bonnet, without the inconvenience of feeding them.

For more than a century, people have dreamed of putting more and more horsepower into their vehicles. Cars became symbols of status, dressed up with chrome, leather, luxury details and whatever extravagance we could bolt onto them. Getting somewhere was only part of the point. Arriving in something other people would look at became a purpose of its own.

It's funny how the new Tesla Roadster might be the peak of that whole era. We wanted more horsepower, and now we're discussing rocket thrusters. Adding rocket thrusters to a can with wheels doesn't make it a better car. At some point, it makes it a torpedo.

And this isn't even the first time someone has strapped outrageous propulsion to wheels to chase a speed record. [ThrustSSC did it with jet engines in 1997](https://thrustssc.com/Latest_News/Issue_197_Lead_Article.html), setting a supersonic land-speed record on Nevada's Black Rock Desert. An extraordinary engineering achievement. Probably less useful for picking up groceries.

Car culture can feel permanent when you're living inside it. But compared with how long people have been getting from one place to another, our love affair with the private car is a recent thing. I don't think it has to last forever.

What people actually need is a way to get from A to B, preferably as comfortably as possible.

In that sense, the future of cars isn't necessarily the fastest car. It's the vehicle that's there when you need it. And that starts sounding a lot like public transport.

Autonomous vehicles might help us get there. I can imagine simple chassis, built around the people inside rather than the person driving, taking you from A to B even when you live somewhere remote. Smaller vehicles where passengers are scattered. A shared ride that turns up when you need it. Bigger vehicles where more people are travelling in the same direction.

I mean, hello, trams. Buses. Ferries. All the public transport imaginable. Some of this future has been waiting at the stop for quite a while.

A fleet of private robotaxis isn't automatically public transport, of course. The version I want would be affordable, accessible and connected to the rest of the network. Otherwise, we've just removed the driver and kept the traffic.

So when [Tesla presents a Roadster with cold-gas thrusters](https://www.engadget.com/2288437/teslas-redesigned-roadster-cold-gas-thruster-option-reservation/), I don't really get the ambition. The technology is very cool.

Tesla has been planting the idea of autonomous taxis for more than a decade. Its [2016 master plan](https://www.tesla.com/master-plan-part-deux) already described a shared fleet you could summon with your phone. And now we get this flex, which adds nothing useful to getting around and will probably be forgotten in a year. Meanwhile, the [new Roadster announced in 2017](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/tesla-set-unveil-next-generation-roadster-sports-ev-after-years-delay-2026-10-15/) still hasn't reached customers. The original Roadster did, and one of those is now [flying through space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk%27s_Tesla_Roadster) after the 2018 Falcon Heavy launch. Another stunt. Brilliant marketing, though.

I'm not even sure flying cars or absurdly fast ones are that exciting anymore. We've arrived at a future with AI, delivery drones and passenger-carrying drones, even if some of those services are still limited to trials. Flying things are hardly in short supply. Is another stunt from Musk really going to make much of a dent? Plenty of people can no longer stand him, and [Alex Gibney's new documentary, *Musk*](https://arstechnica.com/culture/2026/10/review-4-hour-musk-documentary-feels-like-two-different-films/), takes a critical look at the public image he's spent years building. I'm not convinced a car briefly leaving the ground is going to win that audience back.

I can imagine a flying ambulance literally jumping over traffic and landing at the hospital. That, at least, sounds useful. Although if the plan involves the same obsession with acceleration, I'd better never get sick.

In the end, we're just flesh. And flesh has limits. Removing the driver doesn't remove the passenger's organs.

If the ambition is simply to launch a machine on wheels from zero to 100 km/h as violently as possible, I'm not sure "car" is the word we're looking for anymore.

"Torpedo" might be more appropriate.

Maybe we're reaching the end of an era in what a car means to us. I can imagine losing interest in the gold-plated chassis and finding something far more exciting in the journey itself. If we're talking luxury, give me a comfortable seat, my time back, and the freedom to go somewhere without organising my life around a car. That still feels exclusive. I'd like it to become ordinary.
